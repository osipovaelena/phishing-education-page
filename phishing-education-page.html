What just happened

🎣 A routine-looking email led you to a fake login page

You received a message designed to look like an everyday Holmesglen notice, so you’d act on it without a second thought. The link opened a page that imitated a real sign-in screen. On a genuine attack, anything typed there goes straight to a criminal — who can then reach your email, student and staff records, payroll, and anything else your account unlocks.

The good news: the signs were all there. Here’s how to catch them next time.

Spot the red flags

🚩 The warning signs in that message

Pick the scenario that matches the email you received. The numbered markers point to each tell.

FromHolmesglen IT Services <notifications@my-holmesglen-portal.com> 1
ToStaff Member
SubjectAction required: your my.holmesglen.edu.au session has expired 2

Dear Staff Member, 3

Your portal session has expired. To submit today’s attendance roll you must re-authenticate within 30 minutes, or your access will be suspended. 4

Re-authenticate now 5

  • 1Look-alike sender domain. Genuine Holmesglen mail comes from @holmesglen.edu.au — not a near-copy like “my-holmesglen-portal.com”.
  • 2Manufactured urgency and a threat. “Within 30 minutes” and “access will be suspended” are there to make you rush.
  • 3Generic greeting. A real system that knows you would use your name, not “Dear Staff Member”.
  • 4Pressure on a habit. Marking the roll is a daily task, so a login prompt feels normal — that’s exactly why it was chosen.
  • 5Link text that hides its destination. Hover (or long-press on a phone) and the real address won’t match. Legitimate systems rarely ask you to “re-authenticate” through an emailed button.
FromPayroll Services <payroll@holmesglen-hr.com> 1
ToStaff Member
SubjectYour October payslip is ready — action required before next pay run 2

Your October payslip is now available. Please also update your tax declaration in Banner HR/Payroll before the next pay run to avoid a delay to your pay. 3

View payslip 4

  • 1Typosquatted domain. “holmesglen-hr.com” is built to look official but isn’t the institute’s real address.
  • 2Financial pressure. Pay and tax create a strong pull to act quickly — attackers rely on it across every staff level.
  • 3Unexpected deadline. “Before the next pay run” manufactures urgency around something you weren’t expecting.
  • 4Login-to-view trap. You’re asked to sign in via an emailed link to “view” a document. Reach HR/payroll by typing the known address or using the staff intranet — never an email button.
FromCisco Webex <no-reply@webex-holmesglen.com> 1
ToStaff Member
SubjectYou missed a Webex session from your Department Head — view recording 2

A scheduled Webex meeting was held without you. Your Department Head has shared the recording and agenda for review. 3

Sign in to view recording 4

  • 1Display-name spoof. The name says “Cisco Webex,” but the address is a look-alike (“webex-holmesglen.com”). Always read the full address, not just the name.
  • 2Social pressure. Implying your manager is waiting makes you act before you think.
  • 3Blends into routine. Frequent meetings make a “missed Webex” believable — which is the point.
  • 4Credential request. Webex uses your existing single sign-on; a recording link shouldn’t demand a fresh login. Check the meeting in your calendar or the Webex app instead.

Works every time

🔍 A quick check for any message

  • Unexpected and urgent. A message you weren’t expecting that pushes you to click or act right now.
  • An address or link that’s slightly off. A sender or web link altered a little from the real one.
  • Asks for your password or personal details. Legitimate services don’t ask you to confirm these by email.
  • Something just feels wrong. The tone, timing, formatting, or a greeting that isn’t really you.
  • An attachment you didn’t expect. Especially if it wants you to “enable” something to open it.
  • Check before you click. Hover over a link on a computer, or long-press on a phone, to see where it truly leads.

If you spot one

🛑 Stop, check, then report

1

🛑 Stop

Don’t click, don’t enter any details, and don’t reply. If you’ve already entered your password, change it straight away and tell IT — acting fast limits the damage.

2

🔎 Check

If it might be genuine, don’t use the links or phone numbers in the message. Go to the known website or app yourself, or call the sender on a number you already trust.

3

📣 Report

Forward it to [Holmesglen IT — report address / “Report Phishing” button]. You can also report to the ACSC via ReportCyber (cyber.gov.au/report), forward emails to report@cyber.gov.au, and send scam texts to 7726.

Stay ahead of it

🔐 Protect your account

🔑 Turn on multi-factor authentication

The single most effective step. A stolen password alone won’t be enough to get in. Start with your email.

🧩 Use long, unique passphrases

Four or more random words — long, unpredictable, and never reused across accounts.

🗝️ Use a password manager

It creates and remembers strong, unique passphrases so you don’t have to.

🔄 Keep devices updated

Turn on automatic updates so known weaknesses are patched without you thinking about it.
